# 04 — Lessons from Tor

Tor is the most successful anonymity network ever deployed and the most thoroughly
attacked. Every one of its weaknesses is a design decision that was correct for its
constraints and is worth revisiting under ours. Nothing below is a criticism of Tor.
It is the accumulated result of two decades of people trying very hard to break it,
which is the most valuable design input available to anyone building in this space.

Each entry: the flaw, the lesson, what KARST does, and what that costs us.

---

## 1. The relay list is public

**Flaw.** Tor publishes a signed consensus listing every relay. A censor downloads it
and blocks every address in it. Bridges were introduced as unlisted entry points, and
bridge distribution became its own unsolved problem: enumeration through repeated
requests, and active probing where the censor connects to a suspected bridge to
confirm it speaks Tor before blocking it.

**Lesson.** Any global membership list is a block list waiting to be downloaded. The
patch for a published list is an unpublished list, and distributing an unpublished
list to strangers without also distributing it to the censor is genuinely hard.

**KARST.** L5 Membership has no roll at all. Peers are learned by social introduction
at a bounded rate, and each introduction is attributable, so a peer who leaks their
known set can be identified and cut. There is no document to publish and no authority
holding one.

**Cost.** This does not solve bridge distribution, it moves it into the social graph
and declares the harder case out of scope. A stranger with no contact in the network
cannot join. See WHITEPAPER §6.3.

---

## 2. Directory authorities are a hardcoded singleton set

**Flaw.** Under a dozen directory authorities, hand operated, their keys compiled into
the client. They vote on the consensus. Compromise, coercion or sustained denial
against that set is a systemic event, and the set is public and static.

**Lesson.** "Decentralised except for the part that bootstraps trust" is centralised.
A hardcoded key list in the source is a root store by another name, and the same
argument applies to it as applies to CAs.

**KARST.** L8 Witness has no root store and no default authority set. Keys are
witnessed in append-only logs the operator chooses. Two deployments may share no
trust roots at all and still interoperate at every layer below L8.

**Cost.** There is no default, which means there is no safe out-of-box configuration,
which means most people will use whatever their client ships with, which will become
a de facto authority set anyway. We have moved the problem from the protocol into the
distribution, where it is at least contestable. That is an improvement and not a
solution.

---

## 3. The threat model excludes the global passive adversary

**Flaw.** Tor is explicit that it does not defend against an adversary who can observe
both ends of a circuit. Onion routing hides who is talking to whom from any single
relay, but preserves packet timing and volume end to end. Anyone watching entry and
exit correlates them.

This was the right call in 2004. Low latency was necessary for adoption, and the
adversary of the day was not assumed to have that reach. That assumption has aged
badly.

**Lesson.** Correlation resistance and low latency are in direct tension and you must
pick. Onion routing picks latency. Mixing picks correlation resistance and pays in
seconds to minutes of delay.

**KARST.** L4 Mixing is a continuous-time mix with per-hop exponential delays and
mandatory constant-rate cover traffic, in the Loopix tradition, and it is the
**default** path. Volume correlation fails because every node emits at a constant rate
regardless of whether it has anything to say. Timing correlation fails because each
hop independently reorders.

**Cost.** Latency measured in seconds, and continuous bandwidth burned whether or not
you are using the network. See §10 and `05-anonymity.md` for how the low-latency class
is handled and why it is explicitly weaker.

---

## 4. Exit nodes see plaintext, and get raided

**Flaw.** Traffic leaving Tor for the ordinary internet exits through a relay that sees
it in the clear if it is not otherwise encrypted, can tamper with it, and receives the
legal attention generated by everything that passes through. Running an exit is an act
of personal risk. There are far fewer exits than relays, which concentrates both the
observation point and the liability.

**Lesson.** An exit exists because the destination is on a different network with a
different security model. Remove that boundary and the exit disappears with it.

**KARST.** There are no exits for native traffic. You are fetching signed
content-addressed objects (L6) rather than opening a connection to a host, so no relay
can tamper with anything without the signature failing, and there is no privileged
final hop. Gateways to today's internet do exist, and they are explicitly outside the
anonymity guarantee and must be labelled as such in any client.

**Cost.** Everything reachable only through a gateway carries today's risks. During
the entire adoption period, that is nearly everything.

## 5. Onion service directories leaked the address set

**Flaw.** In v2 onion services, hidden service directories could be positioned to
harvest descriptors, so the full set of .onion addresses was enumerable by anyone
willing to run enough relays. v3 fixed this with blinded keys, so a directory stores a
descriptor it cannot itself identify.

**Lesson.** Any lookup infrastructure learns the set of things being looked up unless
you design specifically against it. This generalises well beyond onion services: it is
the same problem as DNS query privacy and as our own index layer.

**KARST.** This directly shaped L15 Discovery. Announcement is an obligation of
**authorship**, not of **holding**. An author publishes an index entry for what they
wrote. A node that caches or replicates an object announces nothing. So discovery works
over authored content while replication stays private, and holding something is not
observable.

**Cost.** Discovery only covers content someone chose to announce under an authoring
identity. Anonymous publication means publishing under a pseudonymous authoring key and
accepting that everything published under that key is linkable to itself.

## 6. Sybil attacks are handled by a small team looking at spreadsheets

**Flaw.** Anyone can run relays, and at scale nobody notices for years. The KAX17
campaign, active from 2017, peaked at over 900 relays against a network that runs
roughly 9,000 to 10,000 total. Before the Tor Project removed them between October and
November 2021, a user had up to a 16% chance of picking a KAX17 guard, up to 35% for a
middle relay, and up to 5% for an exit. The operator ran across more than fifty
autonomous systems including commercial cloud providers, and was never attributed.

The defence that eventually caught it was a small number of dedicated people looking
carefully at relay metadata, over a period of years.

**Lesson.** A structural defence beats a vigilant one, because vigilance does not scale
and does not survive the departure of the people providing it.

**KARST.** Two mechanisms, both from L16 Symmetry. Standing saturates per node, so a
thousand nodes under one owner earn what a thousand independent ones do. Standing is
earned per relationship and does not transfer, so a fresh Sybil fleet starts at zero
and cannot buy its way up. On top of that, L4 path selection requires hops drawn from
standing-disjoint neighbourhoods, so owning both ends of a path means infiltrating
separate parts of the social graph rather than simply buying more servers.

**Cost.** Sybil resistance for *reputation* is not Sybil resistance for *observation*.
An adversary who does not care about standing can still run many nodes purely to watch,
and the mixing layer rather than the reputation layer is what has to defeat that. This
also raises a real barrier for legitimate new operators, who start at zero forever.

---

## 7. The anonymity network is not the leak, the application is

**Flaw.** Tor Browser exists because a general browser leaks identity through DNS,
WebRTC, canvas and font fingerprinting, plugins, window dimensions, timezone, and a
long tail of features nobody catalogued. Most practical deanonymisation of Tor users
has come through the application layer or an endpoint compromise rather than through
the transport.

**Lesson.** Anonymity is a property of the whole stack or it is not a property. A
transport you tunnel a leaky application through provides the feeling of anonymity and
not the fact of it. The fingerprinting surface is proportional to the size of the
application platform specification.

**KARST.** Fingerprint resistance is structural rather than retrofitted. L10 Document
is small by mandate, with no plugin interface, no device enumeration, no canvas
readback, and no ambient network access from content. L12 Agency puts rendering
decisions on the client, so a page cannot probe what it got. L9 Authority means content
holds no capability it was not explicitly handed, so there is no ambient surface to
interrogate.

**Cost.** The platform is far less capable than a modern browser, permanently and by
design. See WHITEPAPER §6.6.

---

## 8. Small anonymity set, because it is slow

**Flaw.** Tor is slow, so relatively few people use it, so the anonymity set is small,
so the mere fact of using it is itself a signal. Several jurisdictions treat Tor usage
as suspicious in itself. Anonymity loves company, and Tor has less company than it
needs.

**Lesson.** If the anonymous mode is a special slow mode, only people who need it badly
will turn it on, and turning it on marks them. The anonymous path must be the ordinary
path.

**KARST.** L4 Mixing is on by default for all traffic, with no user-facing toggle in
the normal case. The anonymity set is every user rather than the subset who opted in.
Constant-rate cover traffic means an idle node and a busy node are indistinguishable,
so even participation intensity leaks nothing.

**Cost.** Everyone pays the latency and the bandwidth, including everyone who did not
need it. That is the price of the set being large enough to hide in, and it is charged
continuously.

---

## 9. Guard discovery, and the cost of fixing it

**Flaw.** Early Tor rotated entry nodes frequently, which meant a patient adversary
running relays would eventually be chosen as an entry and see a given user. Guards
fixed this by pinning a small persistent set, which is correct, and which creates a
long-lived fingerprint: your guard set is fairly identifying.

**Lesson.** Every fix in this space trades one correlation surface for another. Rotation
exposes you to eventual selection; pinning exposes you to set fingerprinting.

**KARST.** Entry is through socially introduced peers (L5), so entry selection is
already not adversary-controlled: an attacker cannot volunteer to be your entry, they
have to be introduced to you by someone you already trust.

**Cost.** Your entry set is highly identifying to anyone who learns it, and it maps
directly onto your real social graph, which is worse than a random guard set if it
leaks. Compromise of a socially close peer is more damaging here than in Tor.

## 10. Funding is concentrated and politically legible

**Flaw.** Tor's development has been heavily funded by US government sources. The code
is open, the design is public, and there is no evidence this compromised anything. It
remains a permanent talking point used to discourage adoption, and a genuine structural
dependency.

**Lesson.** If running the network requires a patron, the patron is a chokepoint
regardless of their intentions, and the perception is a real adoption cost even when
the reality is fine.

**KARST.** L14 Value lets relays be paid directly by the traffic they carry, in a
settlement layer that is a protocol primitive rather than a bank integration, so
operating capacity does not require anyone's grant.

**Cost.** This introduces a payment system into an anonymity network, which is a
notorious source of correlation. Payment and traffic must be unlinkable or the value
layer deanonymises everything above it. This is an open problem in our design, not a
solved one. See WHITEPAPER §6.10.

---

## 11. Website fingerprinting survives encryption

**Flaw.** Even with contents encrypted, the sizes and timing of a page load identify
which page it was with high accuracy against a closed world of candidates. Tor's
defences are partial.

**Lesson.** Padding to a constant rate is the only defence that actually works, and
nobody wants to pay for it.

**KARST.** Constant-rate cover traffic at L4 is the defence, and it is mandatory rather
than optional, which is the only reason it works. Content addressing helps
independently: many small chunks fetched from many peers has a flatter profile than one
document fetched from one origin.

**Cost.** Paid in bandwidth, continuously, by everyone. On metered or battery-powered
connections this is severe, which is why the device profile is exempt and why exempt
devices are explicitly **not anonymous**. That exemption is honest and it is also a
segmentation of the anonymity set, which is exactly the mistake §8 warns about. We do
not have a good answer.

---

## Summary of what changed in the design

| Tor lesson | KARST layer | Status |
|---|---|---|
| No public relay list | L5 Membership | Designed |
| No hardcoded authority set | L8 Witness | Designed |
| Defend against the global passive adversary | L4 Mixing | Designed, unbuilt |
| No exits | L6 Objects | Designed, PoC covers addressing |
| Lookup must not leak the set | L15 Discovery | Designed, announce-on-authorship |
| Structural Sybil defence | L16 Symmetry | Speculative, weakest layer |
| Anonymity is a whole-stack property | L10, L12, L9 | Designed, PoC covers L9 |
| Anonymous by default, not by toggle | L4 Mixing | Designed |
| Entry not adversary-selectable | L5 Membership | Designed |
| No patron required | L14 Value | Designed, conflicts with L4 |
| Constant-rate padding | L4 Mixing | Designed, device profile exempt |
